# ContraPest — 4-vinylcyclohexene diepoxide (VCD) + triptolide

> **Summary.** ContraPest is a liquid contraceptive bait developed by SenesTech,
> and the **first contraceptive registered by the U.S. EPA for rats** (Norway rat
> *Rattus norvegicus* and roof rat *R. rattus*). Its two actives, **VCD** and
> **triptolide**, reduce fertility in both sexes. Efficacy requires **sustained
> consumption** — on the order of weeks, not a single dose — which is its central
> operational limitation [JacobLinnert2022; Massei2024]. (High confidence on
> registration status; moderate confidence on precise dosing figures, which vary
> by study and species.)

## Active ingredients and mechanism

**4-vinylcyclohexene diepoxide (VCD)** is an ovotoxicant. It accelerates the
natural loss of ovarian primordial and primary follicles, depleting the ovarian
reserve and driving females toward an early, menopause-like loss of fertility. VCD
has long been used in laboratory research precisely to model ovarian failure, which
is the basis for its contraceptive use.

**Triptolide (TP)** is a diterpenoid epoxide isolated from the Chinese vine
*Tripterygium wilfordii*. It has antispermatogenic and antifertility effects, and
also acts on female reproductive tissue. In the combined product it broadens the
effect to **both sexes** rather than females alone.

Together the two compounds are formulated as a **palatable liquid bait**, which
matters because rats need a reliable water/liquid source and the formulation
encourages repeated feeding.

## Evidence of effect

- Laboratory and pen studies underpin the registration dossier; SenesTech and
  independent groups report reduced litter production with sustained intake.
- A study on **common voles** (*Microtus arvalis*) reported increased sperm defects
  after 14 and 28 days of treatment and fewer normal sperm at 28 days, and examined
  tissue residues of the compounds [Vole2024]. This is useful both as evidence of
  the male-fertility effect and as a **non-target/residue** data point.
- Reviews note that **VCD + triptolide require a comparatively long exposure**
  (reported as on the order of ~50 days of intake to inhibit litter production in
  Norway rats) versus a few days for synthetic-steroid baits like
  [EP-1](ep1-quinestrol-levonorgestrel.md) [Massei2024]. Treat the exact figure as
  study-dependent.

## Regulatory status

- **U.S. EPA registered** for Norway and roof rats; marketed for use by pest
  management professionals and, in some formulations, consumers.
- Some retail and marketing materials describe formulations as **FIFRA 25(b)
  minimum-risk**; the regulatory characterization has shifted across product
  versions, so **verify the current EPA registration and label for any specific
  SKU before citing it** (moderate confidence — this is an area where secondary
  sources conflict). See [Regulatory status](../regulation/regulatory-status.md).

## Operational strengths and limitations

**Strengths**
- Only rat contraceptive with an established regulatory pathway in the U.S.
- Acts on both sexes.
- Liquid bait station approach integrates with existing pest-control practice.

**Limitations**
- **Sustained-intake requirement** makes population-level effect dependent on
  continuous bait availability and uptake — the recurring theme in
  [Population dynamics](../ecology-and-modelling/population-dynamics.md).
- Effect is **reversible**: stop dosing and fertility can recover, so it is a
  maintenance tool, not a one-off knockdown.
- **Cost and labour** of continuous baiting at scale.
- Non-target exposure and residue questions (e.g. triptolide toxicity) warrant
  ongoing scrutiny [Vole2024].

## Open questions
- Real-world uptake rates in free-living, food-rich urban settings.
- Landscape-scale efficacy — not yet demonstrated for any rodent contraceptive
  [JacobLinnert2022].
- Long-term non-target and environmental residue profile.
